About

I am a postdoctoral scholar in the Department of Neuroscience at UC Berkeley, advised by Prof. Doris Tsao. My research interest lies at the intersection of artificial intelligence and neuroscience, with an emphasis on developing mechanistic interpretability methods to probe neural function. Alongside developing computational models, I design and conduct neurophysiological experiments to study episodic memory and embodied navigation in the primate brain.

Before transitioning to Neuroscience, I received my PhD in Computer Science at the University of Minnesota, working with Prof. Catherine (Qi) Zhao. My doctoral research focused on developing deep learning algorithms to understand human visual attention and reasoning, and translating these insights into computational models.

Research

Despite their distinct origins and learning mechanisms, artificial and biological intelligence both capture the structure of the same visual world. My research aims to bridge these two systems through a reciprocal approach: developing computational models to characterize neural representations and conducting neurophysiological experiments to uncover principles that can inform more generalizable AI.

01 Aligning foundation models and the brain

Foundation models learn rich representations and provide a useful basis for in silico experiments. Nevertheless, due to considerable differences in representation format (e.g., dense spatial features vs a handful of neurons) and available data (e.g., 2 hours of electrophysiological recordings), applying these models to interpret neural dynamics remains a major challenge. My research bridges this gap by developing self-supervised learning paradigm that anchors the two representations into a shared coordinate, preserving the structure developed in the foundation models while captureing the representational geometry in the neural activity.

02 Mechanistic interpretability methods for neural activity

The brain is complex and understanding it requires a systematic investigation. My research develops mechanistic interpretability methods to dissect neural activity from different perspectives, including sparse neural networks for selectivity identification, generative models for stimulus reconstruction, targeted perturbation to examine causal relationships, and dynamical systems analysis to understand the temporal evolution.

03 Neural codes of episodic experience and memory

Intelligence relies on the capability to consolidate episodic experiences into memory and retrieve it to guide future actions. How does the brain implement these computations? I approach the problems with a collection of naturalistic, embodied virtual-reality tasks and high-density Neuropixels recordings in nonhuman primates. Our studies reveal a compositional code in the hippocampus that represents episodic experiences, and discover coordinated memory ``replay'' in the hippocampus and the inferotemporal cortex.

04 Human visual attention and reasoning

Vision is the foundation of intelligence. My doctoral research uses visual attention as a window to study human perception and reasoning. Using eye-tracking experiments and symbolic reasoning models, I investigate how humans allocate their attention during the decision-making process, and integrate the learned insights into visual reasoning models. I also develop autism screening models based on gaze patterns recorded under diverse paradigms.
